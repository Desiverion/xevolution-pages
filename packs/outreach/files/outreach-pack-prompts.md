# Your Crew Writes The Outreach. You Press Send.

30 ready to paste prompts for cold email agencies selling to small local businesses.
Paste a block into ChatGPT or Claude, replace everything inside [BRACKETS], run it.

Every prompt below was written to be used as is. Nothing here invents a result, a testimonial, or a number you do not have.

## SECTION A. COLD EMAIL COPY

### A1. First touch opener

```
You are writing the first cold email in a sequence sent by [AGENCY NAME], a cold email agency that runs outreach for small local businesses.

The prospect: [BUSINESS NAME], a [BUSINESS TYPE] in [CITY]. Owner or manager is likely [OWNER NAME OR "unknown"]. What I know about them: [ONE REAL OBSERVATION, for example "their site has no booking link" or "they have 12 reviews and no reply to any of them"].

Write one email, 90 words maximum, plain text, no images, no attachments.

Rules:
- Subject line under 6 words, lowercase, no exclamation marks.
- Open with the observation, not with who we are.
- One clear ask at the end, and make it small (a yes or no question, not a 30 minute call).
- No compliments, no "I hope this finds you well", no "I came across your website".
- Do not claim any result we have not had. Do not invent numbers, clients, or case studies.
- Write at a 6th grade reading level.

Give me:
1. The subject line
2. The email body
3. One sentence on why this angle fits this specific business
```
Use when: you are starting a new prospect and want the first touch to sound like a human noticed something, not like a template.
You get back: a subject line, a body under 90 words, and one line of reasoning you can check before sending.

### A2. Follow-up 2

```
Continue the cold email sequence. This is follow-up 2, sent [3] days after the first email.

My first email to [BUSINESS NAME] said: [PASTE FIRST EMAIL]
They have not replied.

Write follow-up 2, 60 words maximum, plain text.

Rules:
- Do not repeat the first email. Assume they read it and ignored it.
- Do not say "just following up", "bumping this", or "circling back".
- Add one new piece of information or one new angle. The new angle is: [NEW ANGLE, for example "a competitor of theirs in the same city just started doing this" or "we can show them the list we would build before they pay anything"].
- Same ask as before, worded differently and smaller.
- No guilt, no "did you see my last email".
- Subject line: reply to the same thread if your tool supports it, otherwise 4 words or fewer.

Give me the subject line and the body, then one line listing what I need to confirm is true before sending.
```
Use when: the first email has been ignored and you need a second touch that is not a nag.
You get back: a 60 word follow-up with a genuinely new angle plus a fact-check line.

### A3. Follow-up 3

```
Write follow-up 3 in a cold email sequence, sent [7] days after the first email.

Context: [BUSINESS NAME], a [BUSINESS TYPE] in [CITY]. First email: [PASTE]. Follow-up 2: [PASTE]. No reply to either.

Write follow-up 3, 45 words maximum.

Rules:
- This email must be useful even if they never reply. Lead with something they can act on without us: [USEFUL THING, for example "the three subject lines that get the best open rates for [BUSINESS TYPE]" or "the reason their contact form is probably losing enquiries"].
- Give that thing in the email body, not behind a link.
- After the useful part, one line asking if they want the full version. That is the only ask.
- No pressure, no deadline you cannot back up.
- Plain text, no formatting.

Give me the subject line, the body, and one line telling me what I must verify about the useful thing before this sends.
```
Use when: touch three, and you want to earn the reply instead of chasing it.
You get back: a 45 word email that gives away something real and asks for one small yes.

### A4. Break-up email

```
Write the final email in a cold email sequence. This is the break-up, sent [10] days after the first email. Two or three prior emails, no reply.

Prospect: [BUSINESS NAME], a [BUSINESS TYPE] in [CITY].

Write it in 50 words maximum.

Rules:
- State plainly that I will stop emailing them.
- Leave one door open: a single line saying what to reply if they ever want [OUTCOME THEY WANT, in their words].
- No passive aggression. No "I guess you are not interested". No fake scarcity.
- Do not ask a question that requires them to answer. This email should be closeable.
- Plain text, no signature block beyond my name and the agency name.

Give me the subject line (4 words or fewer) and the body.
```
Use when: the sequence has run its course and you want the list clean without burning the prospect.
You get back: a short closing email that ends the thread politely and preserves the door.

### A5. Subject line generator

```
Generate subject lines for a cold email to [BUSINESS TYPE] owners in [CITY].

The email body is about: [ONE SENTENCE ON WHAT THE EMAIL SAYS]

Rules:
- Produce 15 subject lines.
- 5 under 4 words, 5 between 4 and 6 words, 5 written as if replying to something they already said.
- Lowercase. No exclamation marks. No emoji. No "free", no "guarantee", no "act now", no money symbols, no ALL CAPS.
- Nothing that could be read as a promise of results.
- For each subject line, one label: which of these it is testing, CURIOSITY, SPECIFICITY, or FAMILIARITY.

Then tell me which three you would send first in a live test, and why in one line each.
```
Use when: your current subject line is tired and you want a single testable set rather than 40 random options.
You get back: 15 subject lines labelled by the lever each one pulls, plus a starting three.

### A6. Objection reply: "we already have an agency"

```
A prospect replied to my cold email with: "We already have an agency doing this."

Context: [AGENCY NAME], cold email agency for small local businesses. Prospect is [BUSINESS NAME], a [BUSINESS TYPE]. What they currently have: [WHAT YOU ACTUALLY KNOW OR "unknown"].

Write a reply, 80 words maximum.

Rules:
- Agree with them. Do not imply their agency is bad.
- Do not attack the competitor or imply they are being ripped off.
- Ask two specific questions that a happy client would be able to answer in one line each, about how the current setup is measured.
- Offer something small and concrete with no commitment: [SMALL OFFER, for example "I will send you the benchmark sheet we use, no strings"].
- No pitch. No "when your contract ends". No mention of price.
- Plain text.

Give me the reply and one line on the risk that this reply could read as a sales tactic.
```
Use when: a prospect pushes back with an existing provider and you want to stay in the conversation without arguing.
You get back: an 80 word reply that asks two diagnostic questions, plus an honesty check on tone.

### A7. Objection reply: "not interested"

```
A prospect replied: "Not interested, thanks."

Context: [BUSINESS NAME], [BUSINESS TYPE], [CITY]. My original email was about [WHAT IT WAS ABOUT]. They replied within [HOW LONG, if known].

Write a reply that does two things in this order: accepts the no without arguing, then asks one question that is genuinely useful to them and cheap to answer.

Rules:
- 45 words maximum.
- No "no problem at all". No re-pitching. No "just so you know". No mention of results.
- The question must be about their situation, not about us. Example shape: "Out of interest, is it that [GUESS AT REAL REASON] or is it something else?"
- Give them an easy exit in the last line.
- Plain text, no bullet points.

Give me the reply, plus one line noting whether the question I supplied is a real guess or a fake one, and why that matters.
```
Use when: a prospect says no early and you want the reason, not the sale.
You get back: a 45 word reply that closes politely and may surface the real objection.

### A8. Referral ask

```
Write a referral request email to send to a current client, [CLIENT NAME] at [BUSINESS NAME].

What we do for them: [ONE HONEST SENTENCE, no results claimed unless you have the figure]
How long they have been a client: [DURATION]
What went well from their side, in their words if I have it: [QUOTE OR "unknown"]

Rules:
- 90 words maximum.
- Make the ask specific: who exactly would be a good fit, described by type and size, not "anyone you know".
- Make it easy to say no.
- Offer to write the introduction myself so they do not have to compose anything.
- Do not offer a reward unless I tell you what it is. Leave a [REWARD] slot if you think a reward helps, and say whether you think it does.
- No flattery. No "it has been a pleasure". Plain text.

Give me the subject line, the body, and one line on whether [REWARD] should be filled in at all.
```
Use when: you want referrals from happy clients without making it awkward.
You get back: a specific referral ask with an easy exit, plus a judgement call on incentivising it.

### A9. Re-engagement email for a dead thread

```
A thread went dead. Last message from me was [DAYS] weeks ago about [TOPIC]. They replied once, then stopped.

Prospect: [BUSINESS NAME], [BUSINESS TYPE]. Last thing they said: [PASTE OR "nothing, they never replied"]

Write a re-engagement email, 70 words maximum.

Rules:
- Do not pretend the thread is alive. Name the gap honestly in one short line.
- Lead with what has changed since, on our side or theirs: [WHAT CHANGED]
- If nothing has changed, say so and lead with a new piece of information instead.
- One ask, small, and different from the last ask.
- No apology paragraph. One clause is enough.
- No "per my last email". Plain text.

Give me the subject line and the body, plus one line naming what the reader would have to believe for this to land.
```
Use when: a conversation went quiet weeks ago and you want to reopen it without pretending nothing happened.
You get back: a 70 word re-engagement email and the belief it depends on.

### A10. A/B variant generator

```
I have a cold email that works reasonably well. I want to test it properly.

Here is the email: [PASTE FULL EMAIL]
Here is the current subject line: [PASTE]
What I think the weakest part is: [YOUR GUESS OR "not sure"]

Produce three variants. Each variant may change exactly ONE thing from the original:

Variant 1 changes the HOOK, meaning the first two lines only.
Variant 2 changes the OFFER, meaning what I ask them to do.
Variant 3 changes the PROOF, meaning what backs up the claim.

Rules:
- Everything else stays word for word identical, including length and sign off.
- Label each variant with the one lever it isolates.
- Do not add proof we do not have. If the original has no proof, variant 3 must use a placeholder [PROOF WE ACTUALLY HAVE] and tell me so.
- Tell me what a win in each variant would actually teach me, and what it would not.

Keep all three under the same word count as the original.
```
Use when: one email is performing and you want to learn something from the next test instead of guessing.
You get back: three single lever variants, each labelled, plus what each result would and would not tell you.

## SECTION B. DELIVERABILITY AND INFRASTRUCTURE

### B1. SPF, DKIM and DMARC audit

```
Act as a deliverability engineer. I need to audit the email authentication for a sending domain before we run cold outreach from it.

Domain: [DOMAIN]
Sending platform: [Google Workspace / Smartlead / Instantly / other]
Where DNS is managed: [REGISTRAR OR DNS HOST]

Walk me through a complete audit in this order:
1. SPF: what record should exist for this setup, the exact lookup count risk, and what a broken SPF looks like in a header.
2. DKIM: which selector to check first for this provider, and how to confirm the key is signing outbound mail.
3. DMARC: what policy to start with given we are cold emailing, what to set for rua and ruf, and why p=reject too early will break us.
4. Alignment: how to tell whether SPF or DKIM is the one passing alignment, and why that matters for our setup.
5. The exact commands or free tools to run each check, by name.

Output as a checklist with a PASS condition next to each item, so I can hand it to whoever manages DNS.
Do not tell me a record is correct without giving me the way to verify it.
```
Use when: a new sending domain is about to go live and you cannot afford to guess at authentication.
You get back: a five part audit checklist with named tools and a pass condition per item.

### B2. Domain warm-up plan

```
Build a domain warm-up plan for a cold email operation.

Domains: [NUMBER] domains, [AGE OF THE OLDEST], sending from [Google Workspace / Microsoft 365] mailboxes
Target: [TARGET PER MAILBOX PER DAY] once warm
Tool in use: [Smartlead / Instantly / other]
Timeline I want: [NUMBER] weeks if realistic

Give me:
1. A day by day ramp table for the first 14 days, then weekly after that. Columns: day, sends per mailbox, number of mailboxes active, what to watch.
2. The rule for when to hold and repeat a day instead of increasing.
3. What content to send during warm-up and what to avoid, being specific about the difference between warm-up traffic and real cold outreach.
4. The three signals that mean stop and restart, not continue.
5. Where a paid warm-up tool like Mailreach fits, and where it does not replace actual sent volume.

Be explicit that warm-up timing depends on the reputation the domain starts with, and tell me how to check that starting point.
```
Use when: new mailboxes are being brought online and nobody has written down the ramp.
You get back: a 14 day ramp table, the hold rule, the stop signals, and where a warm-up tool fits.

### B3. Inbox to inbox test triage

```

Body used: [PASTE OR "same as production"]

Tell me:

Do not give me a generic deliverability checklist. Work from the result I gave you.
```
Use when: a test email did not land in the inbox and you need the cause, not a list of possibilities.
You get back: ranked causes tied to the provider, one isolating next test, and the result that clears content.

### B4. Spam folder diagnosis

```
Emails from my domain are landing in spam. Diagnose it with me.

Domain: [DOMAIN]. Sending platform: [PLATFORM]. Volume: [VOLUME PER DAY].
How long this has been happening: [TIME]. What changed just before it started: [CHANGE OR "nothing we know of"]
What the spam placement looks like: [ALL RECIPIENTS / ONLY GMAIL / ONLY OUTLOOK / ONLY NEW DOMAINS]
Content of a typical email: [PASTE ONE]

Structure your answer as:
1. First, the checks that rule out an authentication failure, in the order to run them.
2. Second, the checks that rule out a reputation problem, including how to read the postmaster tools for Google and Microsoft by name.
3. Third, the content and link pattern causes, with what specifically to look at in the email I pasted.
4. Fourth, what to change today versus what takes weeks to recover.
5. A short list of what NOT to do, and why each one makes it worse.

Be honest about which causes can be fixed and which only recover with time and volume.
```
Use when: placement has dropped and you need a diagnosis order that does not waste a week.
You get back: a four stage diagnosis, a split between fixes and time, and a do-not list.

### B5. Bounce classification

```
I have a bounce report and I need it classified into actions.

Here is the raw bounce data: [PASTE BOUNCES OR DESCRIBE THE REPORT FROM YOUR TOOL]

For each bounce, classify as one of:
- Hard: the address does not exist and never will
- Soft: temporary, retry later
- Block: the receiving server refused us, this is a reputation or policy issue
- Content: the message itself was rejected
- Unknown: not enough information in the bounce

Then tell me:
1. Which addresses to remove permanently and which to leave alone.
2. What a rising block rate versus a rising hard bounce rate each tells me about the campaign.
3. The threshold at which I should pause the campaign and what to check first.
4. What to do about catch-all domains that accept then drop.

Give me a table plus a short set of rules I can apply to future reports without asking again.
```
Use when: a bounce report comes back and you need to know what to do with each address today.
You get back: a classified table, removal rules, and the thresholds that should pause a campaign.

## SECTION C. CLIENT REPORTING AND RETENTION

### C1. Monthly report narrative

```
Write the monthly report narrative for a cold email client.

Client: [CLIENT NAME], a [BUSINESS TYPE] in [CITY]. Month: [MONTH].
Numbers for the month: [PASTE THE REAL NUMBERS, for example sends, delivered, opens, replies, positive replies, meetings booked]
Previous month for comparison: [PASTE OR "first month"]
What we changed this month: [CHANGES]
What the client asked about last month: [THEIR QUESTION]

Write:
1. A three sentence summary at the top, leading with what mattered, not with the biggest number.
2. Then four short sections: what we did, what happened, what it means, what is next.
3. One paragraph naming what did not work, without softening it.
4. Any place where the data is incomplete or ambiguous, said plainly.

Rules:
- Use only the numbers I gave you. Do not estimate or fill gaps. Where a number is missing, write "not measured this month".
- No adjectives that overstate. No "incredible", no "massive".
- Plain English. If a term needs explaining, explain it in six words or fewer.
```
Use when: the monthly report is due and you need it to read like a person wrote it and meant it.
You get back: a summary, four sections, an honest miss paragraph, and explicit gaps.

Also give me the one thing most likely to stall onboarding, and the line in email 1 that prevents it.
```
Use when: a client signs and the first week decides whether the relationship starts clean.

### C8. Scope creep conversation

```

### C9. Price increase conversation

```

### C10. Win-back campaign

```

---

## What this pack does not do

1. It does not send anything. Every prompt produces text you review and send from your own platform. There is no sending, scheduling, or inbox management in these prompts, and nothing here checks whether a send is safe before it goes.

2. It does not verify facts. Names, observations, numbers, proofs, and reasons all arrive as bracketed slots that you fill. If you fill one with a guess, the copy will read as true and will not be. Several prompts end by asking what you must confirm before sending for exactly this reason.

3. It does not replace deliverability tooling or legal advice. The infrastructure prompts give you an ordered set of checks and the names of the tools that run them, but the results only mean something when read from your actual sending platform, your DNS, and your postmaster data. Authentication setup, list legality, and consent rules for your market need confirming with your own provider and, where relevant, a qualified advisor. These prompts help you ask better questions. They do not answer them for you.
